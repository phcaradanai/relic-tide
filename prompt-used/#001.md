You are responsible for designing and building a playable digital game prototype called **Relic Tide**.

Treat the following as the game's creative foundation, not as a rigid implementation specification.

Your job is not to mechanically implement a checklist. Act as a game designer, gameplay engineer, UX designer, and technical lead. Make your own decisions whenever doing so produces a more fun, readable, atmospheric, and cohesive game.

## Core Concept

**Relic Tide** is a digital board-game-inspired exploration game for roughly 2–4 players.

Players are explorers / treasure hunters entering an ancient city that periodically emerges from and sinks back into the water.

The city itself is the central mechanic.

Water rises and falls over time, physically changing the playable map:

- rooms become flooded
- paths disappear
- new passages become accessible
- treasures may move with the water
- hazards may awaken
- players may become trapped
- escape routes may change
- previously safe decisions may become dangerous

The board should feel alive rather than static.

A simplified example:

```text
TURN 1

🏛️──🌊──🏛️
│       │
🏛️──🏛️──🏛️


TURN 4 — water rises

🏛️≈≈🌊≈≈🏛️
       │
🏛️──🏛️≈≈🌊


TURN 7 — much of the city is submerged

🌊≈≈🌊≈≈🏛️
       │
🌊≈≈🏛️≈≈🌊
```

The actual game does not need to visually resemble this diagram.

Find a presentation that makes the changing ancient city compelling as a digital game.

## Player Decisions

Players make decisions such as:

- Explore
- Move
- Loot
- Sabotage
- Rescue
- Camp / Recover
- Manipulate the environment
- Escape

These are conceptual action categories.

You may modify, combine, remove, rename, or expand them if the resulting system is better.

One of the important ideas is **simultaneous decision-making**.

Players should often choose actions without fully knowing what the others will do.

Once decisions are locked, actions are revealed and resolved.

For example:

```text
Player A believes B will take a relic
→ A prepares a trap.

Player B suspects this
→ B changes plans and opens a floodgate.

Player C notices both players are distracted
→ C enters the treasure room.

The tide then rises
→ C's original escape route disappears.
```

The interesting part is not simply attacking another player.

The game should create situations involving:

- prediction
- greed
- bluffing
- temporary cooperation
- betrayal
- risk management
- environmental manipulation
- deciding when to escape

---

# The Main Design Principle

The game should repeatedly create stories like:

> "My plan would have worked, but another player's decision changed the city."

or:

> "I could take one more relic, but I may not survive the next tide."

or:

> "Helping another player now may give me a better chance of escaping later."

The goal is to produce emergent situations rather than scripted solutions.

---

# Digital-First Design

Do not treat this as simply a physical board game recreated on a screen.

Take advantage of the fact that the environment can dynamically transform.

The city may:

- flood
- drain
- collapse
- reveal hidden structures
- move floating objects
- redirect currents
- open ancient mechanisms
- change lighting and atmosphere
- awaken environmental threats

Animation, sound, camera movement, environmental transitions, and visual feedback should make each major board-state change feel meaningful.

The player should be able to understand that the world changed by **watching the world change**, rather than only reading UI text.

However, avoid effects that harm gameplay readability.

# World Tone

The setting is an ancient drowned civilization periodically revealed by the tide.

The atmosphere should suggest:

- mystery
- archaeology
- danger
- forgotten technology or mechanisms
- beautiful ruins
- water as both opportunity and threat

Do not feel bound to a specific historical culture.

Develop a coherent original visual identity.

# Technology

Build the prototype in **Godot using GDScript**.

Use Godot as a game engine rather than recreating a web application inside it.

Keep gameplay simulation reasonably separated from visual presentation so that rules can evolve without requiring major visual-system rewrites.

Beyond that, choose the architecture you consider appropriate.

# First Goal

Do **not** attempt to build the entire game immediately.

Create a small but convincing playable vertical slice.

It should be large enough to demonstrate the core experience:

**players make plans → actions interact → the city changes → consequences appear → players reconsider their next move.**

A useful starting scale might involve only:

- a small section of the ruined city
- approximately two players or player + AI
- a handful of meaningful locations
- a few relics
- several environmental states
- at least one way for players to interfere with each other
- a clear risk/reward decision around staying versus escaping

These numbers are suggestions, not requirements.

Choose the smallest scope capable of proving that the game is fun.

---

# Prototype Success Criteria

The prototype succeeds if a new player can play several turns and naturally experience moments such as:

- predicting another player's action
- changing a plan because of another player's behavior
- intentionally manipulating the environment
- seeing the board transform
- taking a risky relic
- becoming temporarily trapped
- discovering an unexpected escape route
- helping or sabotaging another explorer
- debating whether to stay for more treasure or escape

The prototype should create at least some memorable "that changed everything" moments.

---

# Priorities

When making tradeoffs, prioritize roughly in this order:

1. Interesting player decisions
2. Interaction between players and environment
3. Readability of consequences
4. Game feel
5. Atmosphere
6. Visual quality
7. Amount of content

A small polished system that generates interesting stories is preferable to a large game full of shallow mechanics.

---

# Avoid

Avoid turning the project into:

- a dashboard
- an application-style UI
- a static board with menus around it
- a collection of unrelated features
- a content-heavy game before the core loop works
- a visual effects demo without meaningful decisions
- a rigid implementation of this prompt

Do not add systems simply because games normally have them.

Every major feature should contribute to the central experience of exploring a changing drowned city while competing and cooperating with other explorers.

---

# Development Approach

Work autonomously.

Start by understanding the experience the game is trying to create.

Then:

- develop the smallest playable interpretation
- playtest or simulate it
- identify boring or obvious decisions
- improve those decisions
- improve clarity and feedback
- improve presentation
- expand only when the core loop deserves expansion

If something in this prompt produces a worse game, preserve the **intent** rather than the literal wording.

You are allowed to surprise me.

The final game does not need to match the first design you imagine.

It needs to feel unmistakably like **Relic Tide**:

**a competitive exploration game where plans collide inside an ancient city that refuses to stay still.**
